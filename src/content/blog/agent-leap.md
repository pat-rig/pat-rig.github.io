I have never found a good way to say how large a change coding agents were.
"More productive" is true and says nothing. Percentages invite an argument about
the percentage. So this is an attempt to show it instead.

The animation at the top of this page is the whole argument. Watch it once
before reading on.

Before agents, everything I could reach on a computer sat inside one small patch
of ground. A notebook here, a script there, a machine with a GPU in it. I could
get to all of it, and getting to all of it took a day of pedalling.

That was not a complaint. It was a working life, and a good one. The point is
only that the patch had edges, and I knew where they were. Anything past them —
a piece of cloud infrastructure, a web front end, a database I had not set up
myself — was not *hard*, exactly. It was just far enough away that I would not
go there for a single afternoon's idea.

Distance is the thing that kills ideas. Not difficulty.

## The range, not the speed

What changed is not that the work got easier. What changed is the range.

The same afternoon's idea now reaches somewhere I would previously have needed a
week and a reason to visit. The unit of ambition moved. Not because I got
better — I did not, particularly — but because the cost of *going somewhere
unfamiliar* collapsed, and it was that cost, not my ability, that had been
setting the edges of the patch.

That is why the picture is a flight and not a faster bicycle.

## Fuel still costs money

A ship needs fuel, and fuel needs a credit card.

None of this is free. Flying to another planet costs the two resources it always
cost: money, and time. And the time is not only wall-clock time — reaching
somewhere new means learning the concepts along the way, while you build. That
part did not go away and I would not want it to.

So the honest version of the argument is not that the cost disappeared. It is
that **having the range at all is the thing that changed.** The question stopped
being *can I reach that?* and became *is that trip worth the fuel?* — which is a
much better question, and one you answer by making some trips and deliberately
not making others.

We all have cars. We still do not drive around twenty-four hours a day, because
it is expensive and it costs time. Owning the car changed what was possible; the
budget still decides what actually happens. That is exactly where this is, and
anyone telling you the fuel is free is selling something.
